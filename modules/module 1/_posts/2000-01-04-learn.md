### Module 1: Preparing for Evidence-Based Practice
#### Learn
##### Introduction: A Brief History of EBP in School Librarianship
Evidence-based practice in school librarianship has its roots in the medical field (see Sackett et al. (1996) article for more information). The goal of evidence-based medicine, as it was called in its beginnings, was to use the best research evidence to guide medical practice and improve the care and, ultimately, health outcomes for patients. The aim for evidence-based practice in education and school librarianship follows in the same vein – to base decisions about our teaching and classroom practices on what we know about our students and the best research and pedagogical practices.

Before EBP was formalized in school librarianship, school librarians relied on standards and their own professional experience to do their jobs. The focus was on building and maintaining a collection that met the varied reading needs and abilities of their student population, promoting a love of reading, and ensuring the school community could access the materials and resources the library offered. It was focused on the library and what it provided rather than the student and what they were learning.

Even though the term “evidence-based practice” was not being used in the school library field, scholars were laying the theoretical and instructional foundations for school librarians’ use of EBP. Patricla Senn Breivik focused on the importance of information literacy as a critical literacy children will need in the 21st century. Carol C. Kuhlthau and Michael B. Eisenberg created and advocated for the use of inquiry models focused on information literacy and the information seeking process. Kuhlthau’s Information Search Process (ISP) and Eisenberg’s Big6 model further solidified the foundation for school librarians to consider their instructional role and impact on student learning.

These theoretical and instructional foundations for information literacy led the way for scholars like Ross Todd (1995) to study the impact of integrating information literacy skills into the school curriculum and the positive learning outcomes of doing so, helping the field to begin thinking about a framework for how school libraries could measure their impact on teaching and learning. Concurrently, scholars focusing on other types of librarianship (public, academic, health, and special) were arguing for the incorporation of evidence-based practice in the field of information science and librarianship (Eldredge, 2000; Booth, 2000; Crumley & Koufogiannakis, 2001). Jonathan Eldredge formally connected the evidence-based practice framework used in the medical field to librarianship, introducing the idea of “evidence-based librarianship” or EBL in his work with health sciences libraries (2000).

The decade between 2000-2010 brought the formalization of EBP into school librarianship as more studies on EBP were conducted and models created. Todd expanded on his previous work to introduce a 3-part evidence framework – evidence for, in, and of practice (2006) – and further defined this model in his 2015 Knowledge Quest article. While Todd and others were advocating for the use of EBP in school librarianship, Keith Curry Lance began leading major statewide studies on the impact school libraries have on student learning and achievement, providing initial empirical research evidence for the work school librarians do everyday. Kulthau’s work on her Information Search Process (ISP) also provided a means for school librarians to understand how students learn through the inquiry process.

The 2010s saw EBP gaining more traction within the field as more scholars began researching the topic (DiScala & Subramaniam, 2011; Lemieux, 2012; Todd, 2018; ). During this time, scholars researched how EBP was being conducted in school libraries, how it was being taught to new school librarians, the various challenges to incorporating it, and school librarians’ understandings of EBP (Cahill & Richey, 2012; Richey & Cahill, 2014). Researchers saw the value and practical application of EBP as an answer to the field’s struggle to remain relevant amidst the growing use of the Internet as people’s primary information source. There was also a shift in the focus and purpose of school libraries from the library space and collection/resources to what the students learned in and through the library; the field was beginning to see that their role in the school community was less about the “stuff” housed in the library and more about how the library meets students’ various information literacy and research learning needs. As a result, researchers and practitioners began sharing different ways they gathered student data and evidence of their learning to improve their own practice and advocate for the library program.

This current decade has seen EBP in school librarianship continue its examination of EBP practices in the field (Moore & Cahill, 2022) in addition to expanding its focus to consider how the library meets the needs of all learners and helps students navigate and learn in digital environments where a more nuanced and keen eye for information credibility is needed.
